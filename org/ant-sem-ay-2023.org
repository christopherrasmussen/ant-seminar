*** Abstracts

***** /Subring growth in \(\mathbb{Z}^n\)/
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: 2023-09-15
:END:
      [[https://sites.google.com/view/kellyisham/][Kelly Isham]], Colgate University

We can define a zeta function of a group (or ring) to be the Dirichlet
series associated to the sequence that counts the number of subgroups
(or subrings) of a given index. The subgroup zeta function over
\(\mathbb{Z}^n\) is well-understood, as is the asymptotic growth of
subgroups in \(\mathbb{Z}^n\). The analogous questions for subrings in
\(\mathbb{Z}^n\) remain open. In this talk, we discuss the progress
toward understanding subrings. First, we give new lower bounds on the
asymptotic growth of subrings in \(\mathbb{Z}^n\). Then we define a
similar zeta function corresponding to subrings of corank at most \(k\)
in \(\mathbb{Z}^n\). While most subgroups in \(\mathbb{Z}^n\) have small
corank, we show the opposite is true for subrings. If there is time, we
will make connections to orders in number fields. Part of this work is
joint with Nathan Kaplan.

***** /Can we recover an integral quadratic form by representing all its proper subforms?/
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: 2023-10-06
:END:
      [[https://wkchan.faculty.wesleyan.edu/][Wai Kiu Chan]], Wesleyan University

An integral quadratic form is said to be represented by an integral
quadratic form \(f\) if it is obtained from \(f\) by a linear change of
variables. Such a quadratic form is called a proper subform of \(f\) if
the change of variables is not invertible. If \(g\) is another integral
quadratic form which represents all proper subforms of \(f\), does \(g\)
represent \(f\)? In this talk, I will discuss an answer to this
question, highlighting the contrast between the indefinite and the
definite cases. This is a joint work with Byeong-Kweon Oh at Seoul
National University.

***** /Triangular modular curves/
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: 2023-10-13
:END:
      [[https://juanitaduquer.github.io/][Juanita Duque-Rosero]], Boston University

Triangular modular curves are a generalization of modular curves and
arise from quotients of the upper half-plane by congruence subgroups of
hyperbolic triangle groups. These curves naturally parameterize
hypergeometric abelian varieties, making them interesting arithmetic
objects. In this talk we will present an algorithm to enumerate all such
curves of any given genus. This is joint work with John Voight.

***** /Recoverable \(\mathbb{Z}\)-lattices of rank greater or equal than 2/
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: 2023-10-20
:END:
      [[https://berry.edu/academics/fs/fvaldes][Felipe Valdes Gonzalez]], Wesleyan University

Let \(f\) be a positive definite quadratic form over the ring of
integers. We say \(f\) is recoverable if, for any form \(g\) that
represents all proper sub-forms of \(f\), \(g\) must also represent
\(f\). It was proved this year by Chan-Oh that any indefinite quadratic
form is recoverable, but all positive definite indecomposable quadratic
forms are not recoverable. Little is known about positive definite
decomposable forms. In this talk, I will present some examples of binary
lattices which are and are not recoverable and additionally a few
examples on higher ranks.

***** /An exploration of Sato-Tate groups of abelian varieties/
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: 2023-11-03
:END:
      [[https://sites.google.com/site/heidigoodson][Heidi Goodson]], Brooklyn College, CUNY

The focus of this talk is on Sato-Tate groups of abelian varieties --
compact groups predicted to determine the limiting distributions of
local zeta functions. Complete classifications of Sato-Tate groups in
dimensions 1, 2, and 3 have been given in recent years, but there are
obstacles to providing classifications in higher dimension. In this talk
I will give an overview of techniques we can use for particularly nice
Jacobian varieties, and discuss the ways in which these techniques fall
apart when there are degeneracies in the algebraic structure of the
variety. We will explore the various forms of degeneracy in the Hodge
ring, Mumford-Tate group, Hodge group, and Sato-Tate group for some
examples, each illustrating different phenomena that can occur.

***** /How do points on plane curves generate fields? Let me count the ways./
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: 2023-11-17
:END:
      [[https://sites.google.com/view/reneebell][Renee Bell]], Lehman College, CUNY

In their program on diophantine stability, Mazur and Rubin suggest
studying a curve \(C\) over \(\mathbb{Q}\) by understanding the field
extensions of \(\mathbb{Q}\) generated by a single point of \(C\); in
particular, they ask to what extent the set of such field extensions
determines the curve. A natural question in arithmetic statistics along
these lines concerns the size of this set: for a smooth projective curve
\(C\) how many field extensions of \(\mathbb{Q}\) — of given degree and
bounded discriminant — arise from adjoining a point of \(C\)? Can we
further count the number of such extensions with specified Galois group?
Asymptotic lower bounds for these quantities have been found for
elliptic curves by Lemke Oliver and Thorne, for hyperelliptic curves by
Keyes, and for superelliptic curves by Beneish and Keyes. We discuss
similar asymptotic lower bounds that hold for all smooth plane curves
\(C\), using tools such as geometry of numbers, Hilbert irreducibility,
Newton polygons, and linear optimization.

***** /\(\ell\)-adic images of Galois for elliptic curves over \(\mathbb{Q}\)/
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: 2024-03-29
:END:
      [[https://dmzb.github.io/][David Zureick-Brown]], Amherst College

I will discuss recent joint work with Jeremy Rouse and Drew Sutherland
on Mazur’s “Program B” — the classification of the possible “images of
Galois” associated to an elliptic curve (equivalently, classification of
all rational points on certain modular curves \(X_H\)). The main result
is a provisional classification of the possible images of \(\ell\)-adic
Galois representations associated to elliptic curves over \(\mathbb{Q}\)
and is provably complete barring the existence of unexpected rational
points on modular curves associated to the normalizers of non-split
Cartan subgroups and two additional genus 9 modular curves of level 49.

I will also discuss the framework and various applications (for example:
a very fast algorithm to rigorously compute the \(\ell\)-adic image of
Galois of an elliptic curve over \(\mathbb{Q}\)), and then highlight
several new ideas from the joint work, including techniques for
computing models of modular curves and novel arguments to determine
their rational points, a computational approach that works directly with
moduli and bypasses defining equations, and (with John Voight) a
generalization of Kolyvagin’s theorem to the modular curves we study.

***** /A modular construction of unramified \(p\)-extensions of \(\mathbb{Q}(N^{1/p})\)/
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: 2024-04-12
:END:
      [[https://sites.temple.edu/lang/][Jaclyn Lang]], Temple University

In his 1976 proof of the converse of Herbrand’s theorem, Ribet used
Eisenstein-cuspidal congruences to produce unramified degree-\(p\)
extensions of the \(p\)-th cyclotomic field when \(p\) is an odd prime.
After reviewing Ribet’s strategy, we will discuss work with Preston Wake
in which we apply similar techniques to produce unramified degree-\(p\)
extensions of \(\mathbb{Q}(N^{1/p})\) when \(N\) is a prime that is
congruent to \(-1 \bmod p\). This answers a question posed on Frank
Calegari’s blog.

***** /Clusters and non-archimedean uniformization of superelliptic curves/
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: 2024-04-19
:END:
      [[https://jeffyelton.weebly.com/][Jeffrey Yelton]], Wesleyan University
 
Let \(K\) be a field with a discrete valuation; let \(S\) be an
even-cardinality subset of \(K \cup \{\infty\}\); and choose a prime
\(p\). Under suitable conditions, such a set may be used to determine a
subgroup of automorphisms of \(\mathbb{P}^1_K\) whose action induces a
quotient that can be algebraized as a superelliptic curve given by \(y^p
= f(x)\), and such that the quotient map induces a bijection between
\(S\) and the set \(B\) of branch points of the superelliptic curve.
This raises two natural questions: (A) how may one test whether any
given even-cardinality subset has the right properties such that it
induces a superelliptic curve in this way; and (B) in the case that
\(S\) does satisfy these properties, how does the /cluster data/ (the
distances between the pairs of points in \(S\)) of \(S\) compare to that
of \(B\)? I will provide answers to both of these questions by viewing
them in terms of a metric graph associated to \(S\) which encodes its
cluster data.
